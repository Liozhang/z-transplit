Reading Practice Changes What Children Look At

Twelve weeks of structured practice, 240 children, three classrooms per condition

The claim

Structured reading practice does not merely make children faster. It changes where they look, and the change survives twelve weeks after training stops.

What we did

  • 240 children aged eight to twelve, matched in pairs by baseline literacy.
  • Twelve weeks of daily forty minute sessions, three activity blocks each.
  • Eye movements tracked at 500 Hz; comprehension scored by two blind raters.

What we found

Comprehension scores rose 23 percent on average, and the rise was largest for the children who started lowest. Latency measures showed a 40 millisecond advantage in the trained group on sentences with a rare word or an ambiguous referent, and the advantage grew over the course of training.

Why it matters

If practice changes where children look rather than only how fast they read, then practice design is a lever on attention, not just on fluency. That is a cheaper lever than curriculum reform and a more durable one than a single intervention week.
